Surgical Foreign-Object Safety Knowledge
========================================
Reference facts for reasoning about retained surgical items (RSI), foreign-object
purpose, and complications in laparoscopic colorectal / cholecystectomy surgery.
Use only when the question asks about general purpose, retention, or consequences;
defer to the visible video for what is actually happening on screen.

Retention at end of procedure (which FOs normally stay vs are removed)
----------------------------------------------------------------------
- Removed / must be accounted for before closure: Sponge, Needle, Specimen,
  Gallstone, Specimen Bag, Silicone Loop, External Drain (drains are removed later,
  not left permanently), Clip applier and instruments.
- Intentionally left in the body permanently: Mesh (an implant), and ligation Clips
  placed on vessels/ducts (titanium/polymer clips are designed for permanent occlusion).
- Silicone (vessel) loops are for TEMPORARY occlusion/retraction/identification and
  are removed; they are not permanent implants.

Clips (vessel ligation)
-----------------------
- Clips allow rapid, reliable ligation in confined laparoscopic spaces and suit
  small / thin-walled vessels; they are unsuitable for thick or friable tissue
  (which can tear). Titanium is radiopaque but causes CT/MRI artifact.
- Clips are placed proximally (on the high-pressure inflow side) to control antegrade
  inflow; the convention is typically two clips proximal, one distal, before dividing.
- Electrocautery near a clipped vessel should be minimized: thermal necrosis of the
  vessel wall can cause delayed tissue breakdown and delayed bleeding, and excess
  heat may compromise clip seating. (Consequences: tissue necrosis, delayed vessel
  breakdown / delayed hemorrhage.)
- In sigmoid/rectal resection the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) and inferior
  mesenteric vein (IMV) are commonly divided with clips/ligation.

Sponges and hemostasis
----------------------
- A Sponge is used for fluid absorption, gentle retraction, packing, and applying
  pressure. Stepwise hemostasis: direct pressure/packing first; if focal bleeding
  persists, escalate to suture ligation (sponge pressure alone is not enough for
  focal arterial/venous bleeding).

Drains
------
- An External Drain evacuates fluid and is used to MONITOR for complications such as
  anastomotic leak. Drains are typically removed when output falls below ~25-50 ml/24h
  and is serous in character.
- Feculent / enteric drainage suggests an anastomotic leak. A malpositioned drain may
  fail to drain effectively and delay detection of complications (missed early detection).
- Anastomotic leak is a major, potentially fatal complication of colorectal anastomosis;
  drains can help detect it but are neither preventive nor independently diagnostic.

Suturing near bowel
-------------------
- Control bite depth when suturing the bowel wall: reinforcing/outer bites are
  seromuscular to avoid unnecessary full-thickness injury, while careless/excessive
  depth risks luminal narrowing or injury. Sutures may be placed to control focal
  bleeding or repair a serosal tear.

(All facts verified against AORN, ASCRS, and peer-reviewed sources; phrasings reflect
that verification. Scene-specific answers — e.g. exactly which vessel is being divided —
still require reading the video.)
